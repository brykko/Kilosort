# Kilosort4 beats KS2.5 mostly on recall

The evidence since 2024 supports a narrower claim than the Kilosort4 paper makes. On Neuropixels data, KS4 recovers more ground-truth units than Kilosort 2.5, mainly by finding more complete spike trains for low-SNR units. It pays for this with slightly worse per-unit precision, slightly higher refractory contamination, and about twice the runtime. The skeptic's main objection is correct as far as it goes: the paper's no-drift hybrid benchmark injected units that **Kilosort4 itself had isolated**, KS2.5 was run with thresholds overridden to KS4's values rather than its own defaults, and no ground truth independent of Kilosort was used for the KS4-versus-KS2.5 comparison. But the objection does not overturn the result, because the only large independent head-to-head test reached the same ranking with templates not derived from KS4. That study (Buccino et al., Allen Institute, eLife 2026) injected 4,800 hybrid units, and KS4 matched **3,652 against KS2.5's 3,046**. Smaller independent tests shrink the gap. In a SpikeInterface tutorial, KS2.5 tied KS4 on units above 80% accuracy and had better precision. In a biophysical simulation from EPFL, unit yield did not differ significantly across Kilosort versions, although KS4 showed fewer cell-type biases and false units. The GitHub issue record holds many single-session reports of KS4 finding fewer good units than KS2.5, but nearly all involve non-Neuropixels or sparse geometries, outdated KS4 versions or reused KS2-era parameters, and none is a controlled benchmark. Several decisive tests have never been run. Nobody has compared KS4 and KS2.5 using paired juxtacellular or patch ground truth, measured the bias from building hybrids out of one sorter's own units, compared unit yield after curation, or tested KS4 against KS2.5 running at its own defaults.

## The paper's KS4-versus-KS2.5 margin rests on Kilosort-derived ground truth

The Kilosort4 paper uses three kinds of benchmark, and all of their ground-truth units come from Kilosort sorts of International Brain Laboratory (IBL) Neuropixels 1.0 recordings ([Pachitariu et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02232-7)). For the no-drift hybrid (Fig. 3a–c), the authors "ran Kilosort4 with default parameters to get spike times for extracting waveforms". They kept only units with contamination below 0.1, averaged the waveforms over KS4's spike times, and re-inserted 100 such waveforms per recording eight sites above or below where each unit was found. The Methods do not say whether the original units were first subtracted ([Kilosort4 paper, Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02232-7)). This is the circularity the skeptic points to, and it is direct. **Units that KS2.5 could isolate but KS4 could not are absent by construction.** The injected templates also inherit KS4's partition of the data, including any merges or splits KS4 made. The paper acknowledges the general hazard only in passing, noting that hybrid datasets "depend on finding the neurons in the first place". It never discusses the specific fact that its own hybrid was built from KS4's output.

The drift and "full" simulations (Fig. 3d–e, Fig. 4) carry a different and weaker bias. Their waveform bank is **597 units built from pyKilosort sorts**. pyKilosort is the IBL's KS2.5-lineage port. Units were kept only if they were well sampled at every 2-µm depth bin and changed shape only geometrically with depth. The units were then placed over synthetic noise, with rigid plus smoothly varying drift on Neuropixels 1.0 geometry ([Kilosort4 paper, Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02232-7)). That design matches the generative model that the drift correction of KS2.5, KS3 and KS4 assumes. It therefore favours template-based Kilosort sorters as a group, not KS4 over KS2.5 specifically. The only ground truth not derived from Kilosort was the SpikeInterface/MEArec biophysical simulation. The authors used it only to argue that Kilosort2's false positives there come from unrealistically long spikes, and they never ran KS4 or KS2.5 on it. No paired recordings were used ([Kilosort4 paper, Extended Data Fig. 3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02232-7)).

Three further choices tilt the comparison:

- **KS2.5 was not run at its own defaults.** The paper set the detection thresholds of KS1 through KS3 to [9, 8] "instead of their defaults". KS2.5's default is [10, 4], so its second-pass threshold for learned templates went from 4 to 8, which plausibly lowers its recall on low-amplitude units. The non-Kilosort sorters had their thresholds swept; no Kilosort version did ([Kilosort4 paper, Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02232-7); [SpikeInterface kilosort2_5.py](https://github.com/SpikeInterface/spikeinterface/blob/main/src/spikeinterface/sorters/external/kilosort2_5.py)).
- **The metric rewards recall.** The headline metric is the best-match 1−FP−FN score with a cut-off of 0.8, and precision and recall are never reported separately. A unit split in half scores 0.5 and counts the same as a missed unit.
- **False-positive units were labelled with KS4's own criterion.** They were counted only among units that passed KS4's own refractory test for "good" units.

There is no public peer review file. The paper thanks only "the anonymous reviewers", and its supplementary material contains no benchmark details ([Supplementary Information](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41592-024-02232-7/MediaObjects/41592_2024_2232_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)). The 2023 preprint had no hybrid benchmark at all. The KS4-derived hybrid, the ablations and the false-positive analysis appear only in the published version ([bioRxiv v1](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.01.07.523036v1.full)), which suggests they were added during review.

Fig. 4j gives the paper's headline numbers: GT units scoring above 0.8, out of 600 per simulation.

| Drift condition | KS4 | KS2.5 | KS2 | KS3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| None | 551 | 488 | 476 | 529 |
| Medium | 555 | 463 | 461 | 493 |
| High | 502 | 326 | 410 | 294 |
| Step | 463 | 280 | 113 | 262 |

([Kilosort4 paper, Fig. 4j](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02232-7))

Even granting the confounds, the drift-simulation margins are large. Under step drift, KS4 finds 183 more units than KS2.5, and the KS2.5-lineage source of the waveform bank does not obviously account for that. The table also contains a detail the text never discusses: **under high drift, KS2 (410) beat both KS2.5 (326) and KS3 (294)**. In the authors' own simulations, KS2.5's registration step loses units relative to KS2.

## One large independent test confirms the ranking without KS4-derived templates

The strongest evidence for KS4 comes from Buccino, Sridhar, Feng, Svoboda and Siegle at the Allen Institute for Neural Dynamics (AIND). Their paper appeared as an eLife Reviewed Preprint in 2026, rated "valuable" with "compelling" evidence ([eLife RP110170](https://elifesciences.org/articles/110170); [PMC13441294](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13441294/)).

- **Design.** The authors injected hybrid units into 36 Neuropixels 1.0 recordings and 60 Neuropixels 2.0 shank recordings. The templates came from the IBL Brain-Wide Map library for NP1 and from Neuropixels Ultra recordings for NP2. Injected spikes followed DREDge-estimated drift, giving 4,800 ground-truth units. KS2.5 and KS4 ran with identical preprocessing, and no curation was applied ([PMC13441294](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13441294/)).
- **Results.** KS4 was significantly better on accuracy (effect size 0.276 on NP1, 0.408 on NP2) and recall (0.319 and 0.415). The differences were "most pronounced for units with signal-to-noise ratios below 10". KS4 matched **3,652 of 4,800 units against 3,046 for KS2.5**, about 20% more ([eLife article](https://elifesciences.org/articles/110170)).
- **Consequence.** The result persuaded AIND to switch its pipeline default from KS2.5 to KS4 in November 2024 ([eLife RP110170](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/110170)).

On the circularity question, this design matters more than its size. The NP1 templates come from IBL sorts. To the best of the research notes' understanding, those were produced with IBL's KS2.5-lineage sorter. If so, any template-source bias left in this test favours KS2.5, which makes KS4's win more robust, not less. That provenance has not been verified, so treat the argument as plausible rather than established.

The same study also documents what KS4 gives up:

- **Per-unit precision.** For matched units, KS4 "tends to have higher accuracy and recall at the expense of a slight degradation of precision", meaning it "finds more false positive spike[s] in the process". Its pooled precision effect size was nonetheless positive (0.272 on NP1, 0.232 on NP2), so which measure favours KS4 depends on whether precision is pooled across all units or compared unit by unit.
- **Contamination.** KS4's refractory contamination was slightly higher (effect size 0.013).
- **Runtime.** KS4 took "almost twice as long to run".

([eLife article](https://elifesciences.org/articles/110170))

The study has limits that its authors and reviewers acknowledged. Firing was Poisson at 15 Hz, templates were placed uniformly rather than matched to brain region, and whether more realistic hybrids would change the result "remains to be tested" ([PMC13441294](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13441294/)). The eLife reviewers accepted the KS4 > KS2.5 finding without questioning parameter choices or template provenance ([eLife reviews](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/110170/reviews)). The study is independent of the Kilosort authors but not of SpikeInterface tooling, since Buccino co-authored the competing Lupin sorter paper. One more point needs checking. The surname Sridhar appears both on this author list and on the KS4 paper. The research notes treat the two author groups as distinct but did not confirm that they are different people. If they are the same person, this study is partly author-produced.

Version details add small caveats in both directions. A snippet of the PDF lists KS2.5 v2.5.1 and KS4 v4.0.30 ([eLife PDF](https://elifesciences.org/articles/110170.pdf)). The KS2.5 version predates the April 2024 fix for "spike holes", about 7 ms of reduced detection at every batch boundary (every 2.19 s). The KS4 version predates the v4.0.31 fix for spike waveforms misaligned after merging ([#594](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/594); [v4.0.31](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/releases/tag/v4.0.31)). The spike-holes bug covers roughly 0.3% of recording time. It could shave recall slightly but cannot explain a 20% gap in matched units.

## Smaller studies shrink the gap to a recall–precision trade

Every other independent source either tested KS2.5 only lightly or did not test it at all. Where they did, the margin narrows.

| Study | Independent of KS authors? | Status | Ground truth | KS2.5 tested? | Bearing on KS4 vs KS2.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buccino et al. 2026 | Yes (SpikeInterface/AIND team; see the Sridhar caveat above) | eLife reviewed | Hybrid, IBL/Ultra templates, 4,800 units | Yes | KS4 better on accuracy and recall; slight per-unit precision cost |
| SpikeInterface hybrid tutorial | Yes | Documentation | Hybrid, 31 IBL templates, one recording | Yes, at defaults | Tie on units >0.8; KS2.5 better precision, KS4 better recall |
| Laquitaine et al. | Yes | Preprint | Biophysical cortical simulation | Yes, drift correction off | No significant yield difference; KS4 less biased |
| KIASORT | Yes (competing sorter) | J Neurosci | Simulations | No | KS4's lead depends on the metric used |
| Garcia et al. (Lupin) | Yes (competing sorter) | eLife reviewed preprint | SpikeInterface generator | No | KS4 has many false positives after motion correction |

Sources: [eLife 110170](https://elifesciences.org/articles/110170), [SpikeInterface docs](https://spikeinterface.readthedocs.io/en/latest/how_to/benchmark_with_hybrid_recordings.html), [Laquitaine bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.12.04.626805v2.full), [J Neurosci KIASORT](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/46/27/e1594252026), [eLife RP110588](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/110588v2).

The SpikeInterface tutorial is the closest thing to a precision-first view. It injected 31 IBL templates into a drifting Steinmetz NP1 recording. KS2.5, KS4 and SpyKING CIRCUS 2 "all find around 10-12 hybrid units with accuracy greater than 80%". KS4 "has a better overall curve" because almost all its units exceed 50% accuracy, while KS2.5 "performs well when looking at precision" but "has a lower recall" ([SpikeInterface docs](https://spikeinterface.readthedocs.io/en/latest/how_to/benchmark_with_hybrid_recordings.html)). This is one recording and a tutorial, not a study. Still, its pattern matches Buccino's: the difference lies in completeness, not in the count of clean units.

Laquitaine and colleagues at EPFL Blue Brain used a biophysically detailed simulation of a rat somatosensory column. It is the only ground truth in this literature not built from any sorter's templates. **Single-unit yield was not significantly different between Kilosort versions.** KS4 still came out ahead on other measures. KS1 through KS2.5 over-represented rare interneurons by factors of 2.9 to 8.1, and KS3 and KS4 were described as producing no false-positive units where older versions did ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.12.04.626805v2.full)). The study has its own weaknesses:

- **KS2.5 was handicapped.** It ran with drift correction disabled, which matters little in a static simulation.
- **It is unreviewed.** It is a preprint with no journal version found.
- **Its key numbers are inconsistent.** The fraction of well-sorted neurons is reported with different metrics in different sections.
- **Absolute performance was poor for every sorter.** KS4 sorted only about 10–15% of nearby neurons well, a ceiling that applies to every Kilosort version.

The new-sorter papers treat KS4 as the state of the art to beat, and they show where it is beatable. KIASORT's authors report that on KS4's own public benchmark, KS4 "retained a modest advantage under its native 1-FN-FP > 0.8 quality score, whereas the methods were more comparable under an F1 > 0.75 criterion". They also argue that KS4's benchmark datasets "are closer to the assumptions of template-based, geometry-aware sorters" ([J Neurosci](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/46/27/e1594252026)). This is direct evidence that the ranking depends on the paper's chosen metric and cut-off. Garcia et al. report that "Kilosort4 finds many false positives, especially for the motion-corrected recording" ([eLife RP110588](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/110588v2)). That cuts against the KS4 paper's claim that its false-positive unit counts matched other sorters', a count made only among units that passed KS4's own refractory label. Both papers carry developer bias, and neither tested KS2.5. Garcia et al. also flag that the curation tools now standard for real data (Bombcell, UnitRefine) were tuned or trained on KS4 output, which could favour KS4 on proxy metrics.

## GitHub complaints cluster on unusual probes, not on standard Neuropixels

The MouseLand/Kilosort issue tracker contains about a dozen threads comparing KS4 directly with KS2.5, pyKilosort, KS2 or KS3 on the same data. GitHub Discussions are disabled, so these issues are the whole public user record ([repo](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort)). The largest reported gaps all involve unusual geometries:

| Issue | Setup | Reported result | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| [#663](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/663) | Litke retinal MEA, KS4 v4.0.3 | ~1,000 good units (KS2.5) vs ~130 (KS4) | A geometry fix in v4.0.4 then gave "a lot of good clusters" |
| [#964](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/964) | 32-channel 4-shank uLED probe plus a 16-channel probe | KS4 at roughly 50% of KS2.5's good-unit yield, although KS4 detected about 1.5 million spikes against KS2.5's 687,732 | Thresholds raised to 15/12.5 (defaults 9/8), drift correction off |
| [#968](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/968) | Sparse custom Neuropixels map | 30–55 well-isolated units per session (KS2.5) vs "poor" KS4 results | Closed with no final count |

The single comparison on standard Neuropixels 1.0 ([#634](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/634)) found similar counts of good and MUA clusters, but "at least 50% less" total spikes in KS4's good clusters. Marius Pachitariu replied that the result "will depend on your particular experimental prep, probe geometry, thresholds etc." In [#667](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/667) he judged that "both algorithms perform similarly in terms of how many good units they find". **No thread demonstrates KS2.5 beating KS4 consistently on a standard dense Neuropixels map.** The threads also share a recurring pattern: KS4 at default settings detects more low-amplitude events, which dilute the "good" clusters. That makes comparisons of KS-assigned good/MUA labels across versions unreliable, because each version calibrates its thresholds differently.

The tracker does document real quality defects, and several bear on any comparison:

- **Drift correction on small probes.** It is unreliable on probes with fewer than about 64 channels or sparse spacing. The maintainers' standard advice is `nblocks=0`, and Jacob Pennington warned that drift correction on an insufficiently dense probe "is likely to make things worse" ([#619](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/619); [parameters.rst](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/blob/main/docs/parameters.rst)).
- **Over-merging.** The final pairwise merge can combine distinct, rarely co-active cells such as place cells ([#710](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/710)).
- **A changed default reverted.** A clustering subsampling default changed in v4.1.0 was reverted in v4.1.3, partly because of "oversplit units for some datasets" ([v4.1.3](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/releases/tag/v4.1.3)).
- **A split-veto bug in released versions.** A typo introduced in v4.1.5, `len(st2 == 0)`, disables the refractory veto on cluster splits. It is present in every released version from 4.1.5 through 4.1.7 and was fixed on `main` only in September 2026. No issue has measured how much it changed unit counts ([#1042](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/1042); [PR #1043](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/1043)).

Maintainers concede in general terms that "an older version could work better for your specific probe type or brain area" ([#707](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/707)). In individual threads, though, they attribute shortfalls to geometry, thresholds, outdated versions or reused parameters, and they no longer support KS1–3 ([README](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/blob/main/README.md)). The record cannot show that KS4 is worse than KS2.5. It does support a narrower claim: out of the box, KS4 can underperform KS2.5 on non-Neuropixels, sparse or noisy low-channel data until its geometry, drift and threshold settings are retuned. Some user comparisons are also biased against KS2.5. KS2.5 outputs from before April 2024, and pyKilosort, carry the spike-holes bug ([#594](https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort/issues/594)).

Institutional choices split along the same lines. AIND switched on the strength of its benchmark, yet "several projects continued using Kilosort2.5 for consistency". As of August 2025 its pipeline had sorted 789 sessions with KS2.5 and 468 with KS4 ([eLife RP110170](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/110170)). The IBL still runs its KS2.5-derived ibl-sorter. Its development effort has gone into destriping and DARTsort/DREDge motion estimation, and its README and changelog never mention KS4 ([ibl-sorter](https://github.com/int-brain-lab/ibl-sorter); [CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/int-brain-lab/ibl-sorter/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Whether IBL ever evaluated KS4 is unknown. Its spike-sorting white paper was not checked, so its silence should not be read as a negative verdict.

## The tests that would settle the question have not been run

Several gaps keep the skeptic's position open:

- **No paired ground truth.** No study, from the authors or anyone else, compares KS4 and KS2.5 against paired juxtacellular or patch recordings. The KS4 paper dismisses such data as short and anesthetized ([Kilosort4 paper, Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02232-7)).
- **No measurement of the hybrid bias.** No paper has quantified "same-sorter template" bias by building hybrids from sorter A's units and from sorter B's units and checking whether the ranking flips. The bias the skeptic identifies is real in principle, but its size is unknown.
- **No test against KS2.5 at its defaults.** No one has re-run the paper's own simulations, which are public on [Janelia figshare](https://doi.org/10.25378/janelia.25298815.v1), with KS2.5 at its [10, 4] defaults, or with thresholds swept for every Kilosort version as they were for the competitors.
- **No post-curation comparison.** No independent study compares how many units each sorter yields after curation, for example with Bombcell or UnitRefine "good" labels, on the same real recordings. Yet that is the quantity most labs care about, and the place where KS4's precision cost would show.
- **No comparisons beyond Neuropixels in mice.** No independent comparison covers non-Neuropixels probes, Neuropixels Ultra as the sorted probe, primate or human recordings, or in vitro MEAs.
- **No measurement of version effects.** The effect of the v4.1.5–4.1.7 split-veto bug, and of the default flip between v4.1.0 and v4.1.3, on any benchmark is unmeasured.
- **Little scrutiny in print.** No published commentary, PubPeer thread or peer-review record engages with the paper's KS4-derived hybrid.

## Conclusion

The skeptic has identified a real flaw and drawn too strong a conclusion from it. The paper's no-drift hybrid is circular and should not count as evidence for KS4 over KS2.5. Its drift simulations favour Kilosort-family sorters as a group and do not specifically favour KS4. On standard Neuropixels, KS4 nonetheless gets ahead of KS2.5, because an independent hybrid with templates not taken from KS4 reproduces the direction of the effect. What survives is a trade-off rather than an across-the-board improvement. KS4 is a higher-recall sorter that finds more of each neuron's spikes and more low-SNR units. KS2.5 is slightly more conservative per unit. When results are judged by the count of clean, high-accuracy units, as in the tutorial, the KIASORT F1 analysis and the biophysical yield measure, the two are close to parity.

That trade-off implies a practical rule. KS4's advantage is real mainly for labs that use recall-heavy measures or analyse low-SNR units. Labs that curate aggressively for low contamination, or that record on sparse or non-Neuropixels hardware, have little published evidence that switching helps them. The cheapest decisive experiment needs no new data. Re-run the paper's public simulations with KS2.5 at its own defaults, report precision and recall separately, and add a hybrid built from KS2.5-derived units alongside the KS4-derived one. If KS4's lead survives all three, the skepticism is answered. If the lead shrinks to the precision–recall trade-off seen in the independent tests, the paper's headline claim was a product of its benchmark design.
