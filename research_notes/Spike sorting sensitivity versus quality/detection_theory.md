# Signal-detection and estimation theory behind spike-detection thresholds

Scope note: these notes cover classical detection theory as it applies to spike detection, the spike-sorting papers that justify or derive thresholds, limits on detectability, how detection differs from assignment, and whether a "low threshold plus curation" strategy is supported. Several statements about Kilosort4 come from reading the local `ks4-tuning` branch code in `C:\code\github\Kilosort\kilosort\` (paths given inline). That branch may differ from upstream Kilosort4. Items marked **[inference]** are my own reasoning or arithmetic, not results stated by a source.

## Q1. What does classical detection theory say about choosing a threshold (Neyman-Pearson, Bayes risk, ROC, matched filter, CFAR), with unequal costs and a distribution of amplitudes?

### Takeaway
Classical theory says the *form* of the optimal detector is fixed: whiten the data, apply a matched filter (a likelihood-ratio test), and compare the result to a threshold. The *threshold* is not fixed by the theory. It comes from a choice the user makes: a tolerated false-alarm rate (Neyman-Pearson), or priors and costs (Bayes risk). So theory gives a principled operating point only once costs or a false-alarm budget are stated. Without them the result is an ROC curve, a trade-off, not a single optimum. For a known waveform of unknown positive amplitude, the matched-filter test is the same for every amplitude, so a Neyman-Pearson threshold depends only on the false-alarm budget. The amplitude distribution (many small units, few large ones) matters only once you turn to Bayes risk or to the expected count of correct spikes.

### Cited Findings
- **Neyman-Pearson.** To maximise detection probability at a fixed false-alarm probability, compare the likelihood ratio to a threshold. For a known waveform in white Gaussian noise with unknown (complex) amplitude, the generalised likelihood-ratio test reduces to comparing the squared magnitude of the matched-filter output to a threshold — [ISAC tutorial, arXiv 2609.22314](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.22314).
- In practice only the signal template is known, not its amplitude. The matched filter's optimality under a fixed false-alarm constraint is standard (Neyman-Pearson; Kay 1998) — [arXiv 1907.01465](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.01465); textbook: [Kay, Fundamentals of Statistical Signal Processing, Vol. II: Detection Theory](https://www.textbookrush.com/browse/books/9780135041352).
- **Uniformly most powerful (UMP) tests.** A UMP test for unknown amplitude requires a one-sided alternative (for example amplitude > 0). The DC-level-in-white-noise case is worked for positive-only and sign-unknown amplitude — [TU Delft ET4386 detection slides](https://cas.tudelft.nl/Education/courses/et4386/Slides/detection_4.pdf). When no UMP test exists, the fallback is a locally most powerful detector or the GLRT — [WPI ECE531 lecture](https://spinlab.wpi.edu/courses/ece531_2011/5detectionpart2.pdf); [Kay & Gabriel, GLRT vs UMPI](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/ele_facpubs/1334).
- **Bayes form applied to spike sorting (Franke et al. 2015, BOTM).** In coloured Gaussian noise the Bayes-optimal template matcher is a linear filter, f = C⁻¹ξ (a prewhitened matched filter), derived from linear discriminant analysis. Each neuron i has a discriminant d_i(t) = X(t)ᵀC⁻¹ξᵢ − ½ξᵢᵀC⁻¹ξᵢ + ln p(i). Noise is treated as a further class with a null template and prior p(n) = 1 − Σpᵢ. Equating the discriminants gives an analytical threshold, thr = ln(1 − Σpᵢ), which the paper says is "usually close to 0 for realistic spike priors". The authors write that "the spike detection threshold depends on the templates energy, the noise covariance matrix and the prior probability." They set the noise prior to 0.99 and report performance "insensitive to the exact choice of the priors". They define no explicit costs: the objective is FP + FN with equal weights — [Franke, Quian Quiroga, Hierlemann & Obermayer 2015, J Comput Neurosci 38:439, PMC4420847](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4420847).
- **Gaussianity check in BOTM.** The authors tested the Gaussian-noise assumption on real recordings. Noise-segment energies "follow a χ2-distribution with 219 degrees of freedom" and the third moment was not significantly different from zero. Dropping the off-diagonal (spatio-temporal) structure of C lowered performance — [Franke et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4420847).
- **Engineering cost functions instead of ROC.** Obeid & Wolf scored detectors (absolute-value threshold, nonlinear energy operator, matched filter) on synthetic data with known spike times over a range of SNRs. They used a design-specific cost function rather than a single ROC optimum. The absolute-value operator scored comparably to the nonlinear energy operator, and better with a refractory-period check. Matched filters scored poorly only because of their computational cost for an implant — [Obeid & Wolf 2004, IEEE TBME 51:905, Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/685482).

### Inferences
- **[inference] Neyman-Pearson case.** Under H0 the whitened, normalised matched-filter statistic z = (wᵀx)/‖w‖ is N(0,1). Under H1 it is N(s,1), where s is the spike's whitened norm (d′ = s). The ROC is P_D = Q(Q⁻¹(P_FA) − s). One threshold serves every unit, and P_FA alone sets it. This is why a "k-sigma" rule is a Neyman-Pearson rule.
- **[inference] Bayes case.** With equal costs and a per-sample spike prior p₁, the Bayes threshold on z is z* = s/2 + ln((1−p₁)/p₁)/s. It therefore *depends on the unit's norm s*, as Franke et al. state.
  - Example: a 5 Hz unit sampled at 30 kHz has p₁ ≈ 1.7×10⁻⁴, so ln(p₀/p₁) ≈ 8.7. This gives z* ≈ 4.45 for s = 6, z* ≈ 5.1 for s = 8 and z* ≈ 5.9 for s = 10.
  - Unequal costs add ln(C_FA/C_miss)/s. Weighting misses more heavily lowers the threshold most for *small* units.
  - So a Bayes detector would use a lower relative threshold (z*/s) for small units than for large ones. A single universal z threshold is Bayes-optimal for at most one amplitude.
- **[inference] Amplitude distribution.** With a distribution of amplitudes, the Bayes-optimal *single* threshold minimises the expected risk integrated over that distribution. When small units are common, this pushes the threshold down, but only as far as the false-alarm cost allows. This is a well-posed optimisation once costs are set. Without costs it is not.
- **[inference] CFAR.** A threshold set at k × (robust noise estimate), such as k × MAD/0.6745, is a constant-false-alarm-rate (CFAR) rule. It holds P_FA fixed as noise varies across channels and time, assuming the noise distribution's *shape* stays the same. It does not track changes in signal amplitude, which is consistent with Zhang & Constandinou's finding (Q2) that noise-only thresholds behave differently at different noise levels.
- **[inference] Look-elsewhere effect.** Kilosort detects by taking the maximum over templates and over a ±nt time window. Per-sample P_FA therefore understates the event false-alarm rate by roughly the number of effectively independent (template × lag) tests. The threshold needed for a given false-alarm *rate in Hz* rises roughly with √(2 ln N_tests).

### Gaps
- The Van Trees and Green & Swets textbook treatments were not fetched. The Bayes threshold formula η = [(C₁₀−C₀₀)P₀]/[(C₀₁−C₁₁)P₁] and the unknown-amplitude derivations are standard, but here they are restated from the lecture notes and Kay listings above, not quoted from the books.
- I found no spike-sorting paper that states explicit, physiologically motivated costs C_FA and C_miss. BOTM uses equal weights.

## Q2. Which spike-sorting papers derive or justify detection thresholds, and what do they show quantitatively about false positives and misses versus threshold?

### Takeaway
Most practical thresholds are heuristics: a multiple of a robust noise estimate, typically 3–5 σ on single channels. They are justified empirically on simulations, not derived. The one clear analytical derivation in this set is Bayes-optimal template matching (Franke et al. 2015), which gets the threshold from priors on matched-filter outputs. Ground-truth and simulation studies agree that errors are dominated by clustering and assignment (merges, missed sparse units), not by the detection threshold.

### Cited Findings
- **Quiroga, Nadasdy & Ben-Shaul 2004 (Wave_clus).** The paper proposes an improved automatic amplitude threshold for spike detection — [Neural Comput 16:1661, Caltech record](https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:QUInc04); [PDF](https://vis.caltech.edu/documents/9384/quiroga_2004.pdf). The rule as widely implemented is Thr = 4σₙ with σₙ = median(|x|/0.6745), a median-based estimate that is robust to spikes inflating the SD. *Caveat: the PDF fetch failed (certificate error), so the formula and the paper's own justification for 4 rather than another multiplier were not re-verified in this session.*
- **Rizk & Wolf 2009** compared thresholds built as multiples of four noise measures while varying SNR, firing rate and window size. They chose an optimal multiplier per measure using a BMI-oriented metric. The common RMS operator was "least advantageous", and a later paper summarises mean deviation as the best noise estimator — [Med Biol Eng Comput 47:955, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11517-009-0451-2); [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/806712); summary in [Andrew et al. 2013, IEEE EMBS NER](https://personal.ntu.edu.sg/ctguan/Publications/C_2013_Andrew_IEEE_EMBS-NER.pdf). The optimal multiplier values were not retrieved.
- **Zhang & Constandinou 2022 (bioRxiv).** The paper tests empirically whether common amplitude thresholds are optimal across SNRs. The most widely used noise-statistics threshold "can vary in performance across noise levels", so its parameter may need to change with noise. A "spike-noise-driven" threshold was less sensitive to parameter choice and more tolerant of sub-optimal settings — [bioRxiv 10.1101/2022.01.25.477685](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.01.25.477685v1). Numeric optima were not retrieved; only the PDF binary was available.
- **Obeid & Wolf 2004.** Detectors were compared on synthetic signals of varying SNR with tabulated true and false detections. Simple absolute-value thresholding plus a refractory check was competitive — [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/685482).
- **Harris et al. 2000 (tetrode plus intracellular ground truth).** Manual operators showed a trade-off between false positives and false negatives. Error rates ranged from about 0 to 30% depending on amplitude, firing pattern, waveform similarity and operator experience. A semi-automatic method came close to the estimated optimum (best ellipsoidal cluster boundary), at about 0–8%. Bursts and synchrony increased errors, and a single wire did markedly worse than a tetrode — [J Neurophysiol 84:401, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10899214/); [ISTA record](https://research-explorer.ista.ac.at/record/3548). These are *classification* errors given detected events, not detection-threshold curves.
- **Pedreira et al. 2012.** The study ran 95 ten-minute simulations with 2–20 single units. Single-unit amplitudes were drawn from N(1.1, 0.5) and clipped to 0.9–2× the noise level. Twenty multi-unit shapes had amplitude 0.5, "near the detection threshold". Background was 48,000 superimposed spikes per second from 594 shapes, rescaled to SD 0.1. Detection used Wave_clus's median-based threshold.
  - Mean hits were 1.8, 4.13, 6.4, 7.93 and 8.33 for 2, 5, 10, 15 and 20 neurons. Correctly identified neurons plateaued at "about 8–10", and misses tracked y ≈ x − 8.
  - False positives stayed below 3 (2.53 at 20 neurons).
  - Sparse units (<0.5 Hz) were missed about 80% of the time, against about 50% for high-rate units, so they were "twice more likely to be missed".
  - Misses came mainly from merged clusters, with sparse units masked by high-rate or multi-unit activity. KlustaKwik gave "very similar results".
  - Source: [J Neurosci Methods 211:58, PMC3657693](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3657693).
- **Franke et al. 2015 (BOTM).** BOTM raised performance against Euclidean-distance matching "from below 60% to over 95%". Overall performance on a benchmark rose from 83.9% (Wave_clus alone, 8,914 errors) to 97.5% (Wave_clus + BOTM + subtractive interference cancellation, 1,388 errors). With correct templates it reached 99.6% (234 errors). Under the hardest noise condition (0.20 of template peak), Wave_clus made 1,954 errors (72.0%), Wave_clus + BOTM + SIC made 1,160 (83.4%, one unit missing), and correct templates + BOTM made 21 (99.7%) — [PMC4420847](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4420847). So with the right templates, Bayes-optimal matched filtering reaches near-zero error at noise levels where clustering-based sorting fails.
- **Kilosort4 (Pachitariu, Sridhar, Pennington & Stringer 2024).** Scoring was 1 − FP − FN, with a 0.2 ms matching window. Kilosort4 found 80–90% of ground-truth units, against about 50% for IronClust. The dependence of KS4 accuracy on spike norm, firing rate and spatial extent "was minimal". For some other sorters, the strong dependence on spike norm "could not be improved by lowering spike detection thresholds". Thresholds were swept for IronClust, MountainSort4 and SpyKING CIRCUS (which ended at 4.5), but not for KS4. The text gives "[9, 8]" thresholds for KS1/2/2.5/3 in the benchmarks — [Nat Methods 21:914, PMC11093732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732).
  - Simulated single-unit norms were 10 + Exp(mean 7). Multi-unit norms were uniform on 4–10, and 600 low-norm multi-units were added as background — [PMC11093732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732).
- **SpikeForest (Magland et al. 2020).** Accuracy is reported per ground-truth unit against SNR, and headline averages use units above an adjustable SNR cutoff, set to 8 in the displayed table. In a reviewer-requested analysis, SNR and firing rate predicted accuracy for KiloSort and SpyKING CIRCUS — [eLife 9:e55167](https://elifesciences.org/articles/55167). A separate simulated-Neuropixels comparison (eLife 2020, e61834) reportedly found strong SNR dependence for all sorters except Kilosort2, which kept high accuracy on low-SNR units. *This came via a search summary and was not verified in the source.*

### Inferences
- **[inference]** The k·σ rules in Quiroga, Rizk & Wolf and Zhang & Constandinou are CFAR / Neyman-Pearson rules with an empirically tuned k. Their "optimal multipliers" are optimal for a stated metric (a BMI cost, FP+FN, and so on) on a given simulation. They are not universal constants.
- **[inference]** Pedreira's plateau and KS4's statement that lowering thresholds did not fix norm-dependence for other sorters point the same way. Past a point, detection is not the bottleneck. Units are lost because the clustering or template stage cannot separate them, and adding detected events does not help that.

### Gaps
- No retrieved paper plots a full FP/FN-versus-threshold curve for a modern multi-channel template-matching sorter (KS4 or KS2.5).
- Lewicki 1998 (Network review), Rutishauser et al. 2006, Rey, Pedreira & Quian Quiroga 2015, Rossant et al. 2016 (two-level "strong/weak" thresholds), Nenadic & Burdick 2005 and Yger et al. 2018 (SpyKING CIRCUS) were not retrieved within this session's tool budget. Their threshold justifications should be checked separately.

## Q3. Is there a fundamental floor below which spikes cannot be detected or assigned reliably (amplitude-to-noise limits, background "hash", "dark matter")? Are there analytical results on detectability or separability versus SNR and channel count?

### Takeaway
Yes, in two senses.

1. **Detection floor.** Detectability is set by d′ = the spike's whitened norm across all channels. With a Gaussian-like background, reliable per-event detection with few false alarms needs d′ of roughly 5–8 once the many template-by-lag tests are accounted for.
2. **Assignment floor.** Reliable assignment needs a large whitened distance between *pairs* of templates, not just between a template and noise.

The background is mostly other neurons' spikes ("hash"), so it is non-Gaussian and correlated with the signal space. Below some amplitude, a unit's spikes are literally part of the noise model. Many neurons near an electrode are also silent or sparse, and sparse units are the hardest to recover.

### Cited Findings
- **Background noise is made of spikes.** In Pedreira et al.'s simulations, background was built from 48,000 superimposed spikes per second from 594 shapes, and low-amplitude multi-units sat at about 0.5 of the noise-normalised scale, near threshold. Units that are sparse or similar in amplitude get absorbed into clusters or into multi-unit activity — [PMC3657693](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3657693). KS4 likewise adds 600 low-norm multi-units to its simulations to make the background realistic — [PMC11093732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732).
- **Bayes-optimal assignment with correct templates.** BOTM with correct templates reached 99.7% at a noise level where clustering-based sorting fell to 72%. Templates built from fewer than 30 spikes became noisy — [PMC4420847](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4420847). So part of the floor in practice is *template estimation* from few spikes, which affects sparse units most.
- **"Dark matter".** Shoham, O'Connor & Segev 2006 argue that many neurons are far more sparsely active than commonly assumed. They call these "dark neurons", hard to detect with extracellular electrodes — [J Comp Physiol A, Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00359-006-0117-6); [BGU record](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/how-silent-is-the-brain-is-there-a-dark-matter-problem-in-neurosc/). A later review cites Shoham et al. for 60–90% of neurons being silent or very sparse. This is a secondary summary — [Ovsepian 2019, Brain Struct Funct](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/10.1007/s00429-019-01835-7).
- **Sparse-unit penalty.** In simulation, low-rate units (<0.5 Hz) were missed about 80% of the time, against about 50% for high-rate units — [Pedreira et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3657693).
- **Multiple channels help.** Single-wire sorting was "markedly lower" in accuracy than tetrode sorting in the intracellular ground-truth study — [Harris et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10899214/).
- **Spatio-temporal noise structure matters.** Dropping the off-diagonal (cross-channel and temporal) structure of the noise covariance lowered BOTM performance — [Franke et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4420847).

### Inferences
- **[inference] Detectability.** For a whitened template with per-channel norms sₖ, d′ = √(Σₖ sₖ²). Each informative channel adds its squared SNR, so d′ grows roughly as √(number of informative channels) for spatially spread spikes. This is the standard matched-filter result, consistent with BOTM's derivation and with Harris et al.'s tetrode-versus-single-wire finding.
- **[inference] Separability.** For two units with whitened templates ξ₁ and ξ₂ in Gaussian noise, the minimum pairwise misassignment rate (equal priors) is Q(‖ξ₁ − ξ₂‖/2). This is the LDA result BOTM is built on. Two units can each be easily *detectable* (large ‖ξᵢ‖) yet hard to *separate* (small ‖ξ₁ − ξ₂‖), as with nearby cells or one cell before and after drift. The assignment floor is therefore set by nearest-neighbour template distances. A lower detection threshold does not move it.
- **[inference] Gaussian versus actual floor.** At a normalised-matched-filter threshold of 8 (KS4's default `Th_learned`, see Q4), Gaussian noise would give a one-sided tail of about 6×10⁻¹⁶ per test. Even at around 5×10¹⁰ template-by-sample tests per hour this predicts essentially zero false alarms. A threshold of about 6 (tail about 1×10⁻⁹) would already give only tens of Gaussian false alarms per hour across all templates.
  - That Kilosort defaults sit near 8–9 rather than about 6 implies that its practical false-alarm floor is set by non-Gaussian structure: background spikes, template mismatch and drift, and the residuals of imperfect subtraction. Thermal noise is not the limit.
  - This is an interpretation. Kilosort's authors do not state it.
- **[inference] Practical floor.** The "fundamental" floor in practice is not a single SNR number. It is where a unit's whitened norm becomes comparable to the norms of the many distant units that make up the background. At that point its spikes are not statistically different from the hash under any waveform-only model.

### Gaps
- Commonly quoted anatomy (for example Buzsáki 2004: roughly 1,000 neurons within about 140 µm of a tip, only a few isolated; Henze et al. 2000: isolation within about 50 µm) could not be verified. The PDF fetch returned binary. These figures should not be used without checking [Buzsáki 2004, Nat Neurosci 7:446](https://itb.biologie.hu-berlin.de/%7Ekempter/Teaching/2006_SS/buzsaki04.pdf) and Henze et al. 2000 (J Neurophysiol 84:390).
- I found no closed-form analytical result linking detectability or separability to the number of channels *for realistic, spike-composed background noise*. The √(channels) result above assumes Gaussian noise.

## Q4. Do detection (finding events) and assignment (deciding which unit) need different thresholds? How does template matching with learned templates (KS4 `Th_learned`) change the trade-off compared with a universal threshold?

### Takeaway
They are distinct decisions with distinct optima. Detection compares "spike from unit i" with "noise", which depends on ‖ξᵢ‖. Assignment compares "unit i" with "unit j", which depends on ‖ξᵢ − ξⱼ‖. Template matching with learned templates merges the two into one Bayes-style discriminant. It is strictly better than a generic threshold *when the templates are correct*, because it uses each unit's full spatio-temporal energy. But it moves the risk to template completeness: spikes of units with no template are either missed or wrongly absorbed into an existing template. KS4 uses a deliberately incomplete first pass, which misses about 50% of spikes, only to learn templates. The final spike set comes from the learned-template pass.

### Cited Findings
- **BOTM does detection and classification in one step** but scores them separately: detection as FP + FN, classification as correct assignment of true spikes. The threshold for each unit "depends on the templates energy, the noise covariance matrix and the prior probability". Missing templates *lower specificity but leave sensitivity unchanged*, because each unit's threshold does not depend on the other templates — [Franke et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4420847).
- **BOTM and overlaps.** BOTM alone could not separate close overlaps by "a simple threshold operation on the BOTM filter outputs". Subtractive interference cancellation was needed, after which "most spikes were correctly detected and classified" (about 790 overlaps per dataset). Priors penalise overlaps: "the more templates are involved in an overlap, the lower is the prior probability" — [Franke et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4420847).
- **KS4 first pass.** Detection uses a fixed bank of "simple" (universal) templates: 46,080 for Neuropixels 1, built from positions × six waveform shapes × five spatial sizes. Peaks are local maxima within ±20 samples and the 100 nearest positions. "Typically about 50% of spikes go undetected" at this step, which the authors accept for template learning.
- **KS4 second pass.** Learned templates are matched iteratively (matching pursuit, up to 50 rounds) using variance explained, V = 2xWᵀD − x²‖W‖². The aim is to "extract all the spikes above a certain waveform norm" — [Pachitariu et al. 2024, PMC11093732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732).
- **Local code: `kilosort/parameters.py` (this repo).** `Th_universal` defaults to 9 ("Spike detection threshold for universal templates. Th(1) in previous versions"). `Th_learned` defaults to 8 ("Th(2) in previous versions"). `Th_single_ch` defaults to 6, described as "in units of whitened data standard deviations", and is used for single-channel crossings that build the universal templates. The official docs advise lowering `Th_learned` if few spikes are detected or units drop in and out, lowering `Th_universal` to find more units, and changing each "by 1 or 2 at a time" — [Kilosort4 parameter docs](https://kilosort.readthedocs.io/en/latest/parameters.html).
- **Local code: `kilosort/template_matching.py::run_matching` (this branch).**
  - `B` is the projection of the whitened, PC-filtered data onto each learned template U. `nm = ‖U‖²`.
  - The statistic is `Cf = relu(B)**2 / nm`, the squared projection onto the *unit-norm* template, which equals the maximised variance explained (2xb − x²‖U‖² at x = b/‖U‖²).
  - A spike is accepted where the maximum over templates and over a ±nt window exceeds `Th**2`, that is where the normalised matched-filter output exceeds `Th_learned` in whitened SD units.
  - Amplitude is `B/nm`. The matched template is then subtracted and the statistic recomputed nearby (lines 232–288).
  - `kilosort/spikedetect.py` applies `As > ops['Th_universal']` in the same way for the universal-template pass (line 182).

### Inferences
- **[inference] What `Th_learned` is in detection-theory terms.** It is a Neyman-Pearson / GLRT threshold on the normalised matched-filter statistic z, one-sided because of `relu`. Under white noise, z is about N(0,1) under H0 and about N(s,1) for a spike of whitened norm s (in the template's subspace). So `Th_learned` = 8 means roughly 50% detection for spikes whose whitened norm *along their own template* is about 8. Detection is near 100% for norm ≥ 10 and near 0 for norm ≤ 6. The threshold is a single CFAR-type value for all templates, not the norm-dependent Bayes threshold of Franke et al. (z* = s/2 + ln(p₀/p₁)/s).
  - Small templates (s ≈ 8–10) have z* ≈ 5–6 under Bayes with realistic rates, well below 8. So KS4's default is *conservative for small units* relative to an equal-cost Bayes rule.
  - For large units it is effectively irrelevant, since they far exceed 8 either way.
- **[inference] Why the learned pass can use a lower threshold than the universal pass (8 vs 9).** Matching against the exact template captures more of the spike's energy than the nearest of a generic bank, so d′ is higher at the same noise level. It also faces fewer effective tests: hundreds of learned templates rather than 46,080 universal ones. Both raise power at a fixed false-alarm rate.
- **[inference] Different optima for detection and assignment.** The detection threshold sets the spike-versus-noise operating point. Assignment in template matching is "argmax over templates", which is Bayes-optimal under equal priors only if every active unit has a template.
  - With an incomplete template set, lowering `Th_learned` increases the share of accepted events from *unmodelled* units (hash) that get absorbed into the nearest template. This is the specificity loss Franke et al. describe.
  - So the assignment-quality cost of a lower `Th_learned` rises fastest for templates that have near neighbours in the background, typically small, spatially spread units.
- **[inference] Generic versus learned thresholds.** A generic single-channel or universal threshold has lower power, because templates mismatch the true waveforms. But it cannot be biased toward existing templates. A learned-template threshold has higher power for modelled units and is blind, or prone to misassignment, for unmodelled ones. This is why KS4 adds a second, re-clustering round: the ablation showed that turning off reclustering "resulted in more misses" ([PMC11093732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732)).

### Gaps
- The KS4 paper as fetched states no numeric `Th_learned` and no KS4 threshold sweep. The mapping of `Th_learned` to the matched-filter z-statistic above comes from the local branch code and could differ upstream. The paper text says matches "use the average norm of the template", which may describe a different variant.
- I found no published per-unit Bayes-threshold variant of Kilosort (thresholds scaled with template norm and firing rate) and no evaluation of one.

## Q5. Does theory say a low detection threshold plus strict later curation dominates a high threshold (information lost at detection is unrecoverable; false alarms can be removed later)?

### Takeaway
Partly. Information theory (the data-processing inequality) guarantees that events discarded at detection cannot be recovered downstream. So a lower threshold never reduces the information *available*, and decoding studies confirm that discarded low-quality events carry signal. But "dominates" holds only if the later stage really is a good classifier. Extra false alarms are not free:
- they contaminate template estimates;
- they get absorbed into real units, which in a template matcher lowers specificity;
- they bias correlation and rate estimates;
- they cost curation effort.

Empirically, lowering detection thresholds does not fix units lost to clustering. The best-supported practice is to detect liberally but keep event labels graded, with sorted, multi-unit and hash events kept separate, rather than to detect liberally and hard-assign everything.

### Cited Findings
- **Todorova et al. 2014 (motor-cortex decoding).** Expert sorting discarded about 80% (monkey A) and 50% (monkey B) of threshold crossings as hash. Decoding the unsorted crossings beat expert-sorted neurons on 63% of trials. Adding the hash back improved on expert sorting for every decoder and monkey, for example median RMSE 0.189 → 0.157 m/s (A) and 0.311 → 0.218 (B). The authors' conclusion was "the best approach is to spike-sort, but keep the hash". Monkey B's thresholds were 3 SD. The authors note "different choices of thresholds might re-balance the relative efficiencies" but leave threshold analysis "beyond the scope" — [J Neural Eng, PMC4454741](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4454741).
- **Ventura & Gerkin 2012.** Associations between cells carry information about spike identities, and ignoring that information during sorting biases correlation estimates. An "ensemble" sorting approach is proposed to remove the bias even for poorly isolated cells — [PNAS 109:7230, PDF](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~vventura/SS.pdf). The companion work argues that waveform-only sorting biases rate-code estimates — [Ventura 2009, PNAS](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~vventura/6921.full.pdf). Related model-based approach: [Pillow et al. 2013, PLoS One, PMC3643981](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3643981/). *Only abstracts and summaries were seen for these.*
- **Lowering thresholds does not rescue clustering failures.** For several sorters, accuracy's dependence on spike norm "could not be improved by lowering spike detection thresholds" — [Pachitariu et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11093732). In simulation, misses came mainly from merged clusters, and false positives stayed below 3 per recording — [Pedreira et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3657693).
- **Template errors.** With an incomplete template set, BOTM's specificity falls while sensitivity is unchanged. Templates from fewer than 30 spikes are noisy — [Franke et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4420847). False alarms that enter template learning therefore degrade the later classifier rather than simply being removable.
- **Operator trade-off.** Harris et al. found that human cluster cutting trades false positives against false negatives, with errors of 0–30%, against about 0–8% for the semi-automatic method near the estimated optimum — [PubMed 10899214](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10899214/). Curation is itself a noisy classifier with its own ROC.

### Inferences
- **[inference] Data-processing inequality.** If S is the spike train and D the detected events, any later curated set C = g(D) satisfies I(S; C) ≤ I(S; D). Lowering the threshold enlarges D, so it weakly raises the information ceiling, and missed events are unrecoverable without re-detection.
  - The ceiling is not the outcome. Real pipelines are not information-preserving: they hard-assign, learn templates from detected events, and use fixed cluster-level metrics.
  - So "low threshold + curation" dominates only if the curation stage is close to Bayes-optimal *and* the extra false alarms do not degrade template learning.
- **[inference] KS4 already detects in stages.** Liberal detection is useful for *template discovery* (`Th_universal`, `Th_single_ch`), where misses and false alarms both affect which units exist. Conservative detection helps *final assignment* (`Th_learned`), where a false alarm becomes a contaminating spike in a real unit.
  - The two thresholds are best tuned separately: lower `Th_universal` to find more units, and set `Th_learned` by an explicit false-alarm or contamination budget per unit.
  - This mirrors the Kilosort docs' advice ([parameters page](https://kilosort.readthedocs.io/en/latest/parameters.html)). The cost-based framing is mine.
- **[inference] What "something to optimise" looks like.** A principled operating point exists once an objective is fixed. Examples:
  - (a) maximise the number of units passing a contamination bound, such as refractory-period violation ≤ x% or matched-random-control S;
  - (b) minimise FP + FN on hybrid ground truth, the metric KS4 and BOTM use;
  - (c) maximise decoding or information for the downstream analysis.
  
  Each objective gives a different optimum, and theory (Bayes risk) says the optimal per-unit threshold should fall as firing rate rises and as miss cost rises relative to false-alarm cost.
  - "More spikes versus cleaner spikes" is therefore not arbitrary once the cost ratio is stated. It is arbitrary only while the cost ratio is left implicit.
  - For a fixed objective, a sweep on hybrid data (FP and FN per unit against `Th_learned`) is the empirical ROC that the theory says to read the operating point from.
- **[inference] Keep graded labels.** Detecting liberally and keeping per-spike matched-filter scores (KS4 already stores `th_amps`, the √ of the detection statistic, in `run_matching`) lets later analyses apply their own threshold. This is the "keep the hash" result in Kilosort form.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that formally proves or disproves "low threshold + curation dominates" for a template-matching sorter under realistic, spike-composed noise. The claim rests on the data-processing inequality plus decoding evidence from threshold-crossing BMIs, not on spike-sorting ground truth.
- I found no study quantifying how false alarms in the template-learning stage (`Th_universal`) propagate to final-unit contamination in KS4.
