# Downstream scientific consequences of spike-sorting errors, and whether they define a task-dependent "right" sensitivity

Scope: how missed spikes (false negatives, FN), false positives (FP), misassignment between units, and over-splitting or merging bias the analyses people run, and whether this implies a task-dependent operating point for sorter sensitivity (relevant to KS4 vs KS2.5 tuning on hippocampal data scored by position decoding).

Note on access: several full texts were blocked (PMC reCAPTCHA, 403s). Where a number comes from an abstract or search-engine summary and not the full text, this is flagged as "abstract-level" or "unverified".

## 1. False positives vs false negatives: effect on correlation, synchrony, tuning and rate estimates

### Takeaway
For pairwise correlation and synchrony, sorting errors bias estimates in ways that do not shrink with more data. Contamination (misassignment) between neighbouring units is the worst case, because it can bias correlations either way depending on firing statistics. Pure FN and pure FP from independent noise mostly dilute or deflate significance. Among the two, FNs reduce synchrony significance more (Pazienti & Grün). Overlapping-spike failures (shadowing) put an artificial dip at zero lag in cross-correlograms. So for synchrony and connectivity work the "safe" direction is not simply "be conservative": missing spikes also costs significance, and the dominant hazard is cross-unit contamination.

### Cited Findings
- **Ventura & Gerkin 2012 (PNAS 109:7230):** when neurons are sorted independently, coincidence and correlation estimates are biased and *inconsistent*: "the bias does not disappear in large samples." The bias vanishes only with perfect isolation, or when the cells are Poisson, independent and equally firing. — [Ventura & Gerkin 2012, PMC3358902](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3358902)
- In their simulations (two cells, one electrode, 1-D Gaussian waveform features, λ=10 Hz, 25 ms bins), misclassification pulls Poisson-cell correlations toward zero. It biases bursty cells positive and periodic cells negative. With 5–10% misclassification, pairs with very different true correlations (-0.05, 0, 0.1) can give the same estimate. The authors note that "a misclassification rate of 5% or more is not rare." — [Ventura & Gerkin 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3358902)
- In the same simulations, bias grows with waveform-cluster overlap, coupling strength and firing-rate imbalance between the cells. At 17.3% misclassification (λA=4 Hz, λB=12 Hz), power to detect a true coupling was about 35% with conventional sorting versus about 90% with their proposed "ensemble sorting." Ensemble sorting computes the joint posterior over spike identities and uses it, without hard assignments, to estimate coincidences. It was unbiased in all simulated configurations. Regime: simulation only; no real-data application. — [Ventura & Gerkin 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3358902)
- Ventura & Gerkin also cite prior work showing that poor isolation biases firing-rate estimates unless the confused cells have equal rates. They say the same joint-sorting logic applies to tuning curves but give no new tuning numbers. — [Ventura & Gerkin 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3358902)
- **Ventura 2009 (tuning):** I could not retrieve the 2009 papers directly. A secondary source summarises them as showing that imperfect sorting biases receptive-field/tuning estimates and biases and inflates the variance of decoding. It also says tuning information should be built into sorting to get unbiased, consistent tuning-function estimates. — [NeurIPS 2023 paper citing Ventura 2009](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/f499387f191d6be56e68966181095878-Paper-Conference.pdf); [Ventura 2008, Neural Comput 20:923 "Spike train decoding without spike sorting"](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~vventura/SS.pdf)
- **Pazienti & Grün 2006 (J Comput Neurosci 21:329), abstract-level:** they model FN and FP as independent fractions of each neuron's rate. FPs raise both observed and expected coincidence counts, and FNs lower both. Both reduce the significance of unitary events, but FNs have the larger effect. The authors conclude that conservative sorting strategies are more likely to lose synchrony significance. Regime: analytical and Poisson-model. — [Pazienti & Grün 2006 listing](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/robustness-of-the-significance-of-spike-synchrony-with-respect-to-sorting-errors/812062707468468225-11386); [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360855570964730752)
- **Bar-Gad et al. 2001 (J Neurosci Methods 107:1), abstract-level:** a sorter's failure to resolve overlapping spikes, which they call the "shadowing effect," causes the familiar decrease in cross-correlation at zero offset. It also distorts auto- and cross-correlograms, and a unit's autocorrelogram can show firing features of other neurons on the same electrode. The effect matters most where firing rates or synchrony are high. Data: monkey globus pallidus. — [HUJI CRIS record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/failure-in-identification-of-overlapping-spikes-from-multiple-neu/); [ELSC summary](https://elsc.huji.ac.il/?p=9285)
- **Cohen & Kohn 2011 (Nat Neurosci 14:811), review, abstract-level:** spike-sorting conventions are one of several methodological factors (with response strength, counting window and internal state) that can systematically bias measured noise correlations and explain discrepancies between studies. I could not check the full text's treatment of contamination. — [PMC3586814](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3586814)
- **Pillow, Shlens, Chichilnisky & Simoncelli 2013 (PLoS ONE):** a model-based spike sorter (binary pursuit) designed to remove correlation artifacts in multi-neuron recordings. This follows the same diagnosis: greedy or independent sorting of overlapping spikes creates correlation artifacts. — [PMC3643981](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3643981/)

### Inferences
- For CCG-based connectivity and synchrony, the harmful error types rank roughly as follows: (i) cross-contamination between near-synchronous neighbours, which creates a spurious or distorted zero-lag peak or trough; (ii) failure to resolve overlaps, which creates a zero-lag trough; (iii) FN, which costs significance; (iv) independent-noise FP, which dilutes. A more sensitive setting that adds marginal units is safe for these analyses only if those units are not contaminated by, or split from, their neighbours. This links to the user's earlier duplicate and near-synchronous-neighbour audit, where a 0.3–0.5 ms pile-up was mostly near-synchronous neighbours.
- Bias that does not vanish with sample size means longer recordings do not fix sorting-induced correlation errors. Only better isolation or joint modelling does.
- Rate and tuning bias from contamination scales with the rate imbalance between the confused units. A low-rate unit contaminated by a high-rate neighbour gets the most distorted tuning curve or place field.

### Gaps
- I could not access the full texts of Ventura 2009 (tuning bias) or Pazienti & Grün 2006 (tolerance numbers, e.g. what % FN/FP is tolerable).
- I found no study that quantifies how much place-field width or position information is biased by contamination at Kilosort-typical error rates in hippocampal recordings.

## 2. Decoding without sorting or with threshold crossings: how performance varies with threshold, and is the optimum below the isolation-optimal threshold?

### Takeaway
For decoding, a consistent finding across BMI (motor cortex, Utah arrays) and hippocampal (tetrode, clusterless) work is that throwing away poorly isolated spikes ("hash") hurts. Keeping every detected spike, with waveform features (clusterless), matches or beats decoding from sorted units. Decoding performance is fairly flat over a range of detection thresholds (about -3 to -4.5 x RMS on Utah arrays), with the best value depending on noise level. One animal peaked at the lowest threshold tested, which suggests the decoding optimum sits at or below conventional detection thresholds and below the threshold that maximizes isolation. Sorting still helps when a channel carries units with genuinely different tuning, so the optimum is "keep everything, and separate where separable," not "don't sort."

### Cited Findings
- **Christie et al. 2015 (J Neural Eng 12:016009); 58 sessions, 2 macaques, Utah arrays, center-out reaching, offline Naive Bayes and linear regression:** the highest thresholding performance came from thresholds between -3 and -4.5 x V_RMS (tested from -3 to -18 x V_RMS). Best Naive Bayes threshold was -4.5 x RMS for monkey L (90% correct) and -3 x RMS for monkey I (89%; chance about 14%). Best correlation was at -4 x RMS for L (ρ=0.92) and -3 x RMS for I (ρ=0.89). — [Christie et al. 2015, eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0ww0z6xm)
- Christie et al., fixed-voltage sweep: monkey I was best at -10 µV, "the lowest possible value." Its threshold analyses "did not contain a peak," meaning performance kept improving as the threshold dropped. Monkey L peaked at -70 µV (Naive Bayes) and -50 µV (correlation). The authors attribute L's higher optimum to an older, noisier array. They hypothesise about -3 x RMS is best for low-noise arrays and about -4.5 x RMS for noisy ones, and say thresholds between -3 and -5 x RMS "result in similar performances." — [Christie et al. 2015](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0ww0z6xm)
- Christie et al., sorted vs threshold: sorted data beat thresholded data in one animal but not the other. Mean Naive Bayes accuracy for sorted data was 88.5% and changed by about 5% when thresholded; mean correlation was 0.92 and changed by 0.015. — [Christie et al. 2015](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0ww0z6xm)
- Christie et al., waveform quality classes: multi-unit waveforms not clearly separated from the hash (category 2) gave the *highest* performance of any single category. Adding well-isolated single units to categories 2+3 improved performance only minimally, though significantly. Waveforms removed during manual sorting for lacking a bipolar shape "occasionally contained useful information," and "it is nearly impossible to visually distinguish between useful hash and noise." Citing Wood et al., they note manual sorting error rates averaging 23% FP and 30% FN. — [Christie et al. 2015](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0ww0z6xm)
- **Todorova et al. 2014 (J Neural Eng 11:056005); 2 macaques, PMv (73 electrodes) and M1 (96 electrodes), offline:** decoding from sorted units beat raw threshold crossings. Median velocity RMSE for monkey B with ML/OLE was 0.255 m/s unsorted vs 0.218–0.228 m/s for sorting variants, and 0.199 m/s for a joint waveform-plus-tuning model. Reported efficiency gains over unsorted were 25–27% (A, ML/OLE), 5–12% (A, Kalman), and up to 58% (B, JOINT, ML/OLE) and 20% (B, Kalman). They found that *discarding* unassigned crossings degrades decoding. Thresholds were fixed (expert-set for sortability, or 3 SD), and the authors say different thresholds "might re-balance" the comparison but did not test this. — [Todorova et al. 2014, PMC4454741](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4454741)
- Todorova et al. also note that Fraser et al. 2009 found online decoding with expert sorting (discarding poorly isolated spikes) was about as accurate as threshold-crossing decoding, and that closed-loop adaptation may mask differences. — [Todorova et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4454741); [Fraser et al. 2009, J Neural Eng 6:055004](https://schwartzlab.sni.pitt.edu/pub/without_spike_sorting.pdf)
- **Kloosterman et al. 2014 (J Neurophysiol); rat CA1 tetrodes, 12 datasets from 8 rats, 250 ms bins, position decoding:** the clusterless "spike feature" decoder had a mean median error of 4.9 cm (range 3.2–6.2 cm). It averaged 14% better than a sorted decoder using isolated clusters plus hash (P=0.0068), 7% better than isolated clusters only (P=0.043), and 64% better than hash alone. Against sorted clusters *without quality criteria*, the two were comparable (P=0.90). — [Kloosterman et al., PMC3921373](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3921373)
- Kloosterman et al.: for both decoders, dropping either the isolated spikes or the hash significantly worsened decoding. The sorted decoder also got worse without hash (P=0.0024). The clusterless advantage grew to 38% with single electrodes, where sorting is harder. They report no error figures for raising the amplitude threshold; it was suggested only to reduce computation. — [Kloosterman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3921373)
- **Ghahari & Eden 2025 (arXiv 2512.06280); rat CA1/CA3 tetrodes, W-maze, 33 ms bins:** on the full dataset, clusterless decoding had rMSE 15.93 vs 31.97 for sorted, a 95% HPD width of 20.61 vs 44.85, and coverage of 88% vs 83%. Restricted to the same spikes the sorted model used, clusterless improved only slightly (rMSE 29.61). Spikes discarded by sorting carried little information on their own, but accounted for about 37–48% of the incremental gains (48% of entropy reduction, 43% of rMSE reduction). Gains were largest where sortable neurons were sparse. About 99% of low-amplitude spikes were discarded by sorting. — [Ghahari & Eden 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06280v1)
- **Deng et al. 2015 (marked point process filter, Neural Comput):** extended clusterless decoding to adaptive, real-time filtering and compared it with a sort-then-decode pipeline. I could not retrieve its numbers. **Denovellis et al. 2021 (eLife 10:e64505)** used both sorted and clusterless decoding of hippocampal replay, and reviewers asked for checks that rates during SWRs were comparable between the two. — [Deng et al. PMC4805376](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4805376); [Denovellis et al. 2021](https://elifesciences.org/articles/64505/figures)
- **Trautmann et al. 2019 (Neuron 103:292); macaque PMd/M1, Utah arrays plus Neuropixels:** threshold crossings reproduced the population-level conclusions of three studies. Variance in the jPCA plane was 23% for sorted data vs 23%, 23% and 21% at -3.5, -4.0 and -4.5 x RMS. The weakest replication was the output-null/potent ratio, 5.6 sorted vs 3.84 at -3.5 x RMS; significance was lost at stricter thresholds (p=0.269 at -4.0, p=0.554 at -4.5). That is, *more permissive* thresholds preserved the result better. Lower thresholds such as -1 x RMS were proposed but not tested. — [Trautmann et al. 2019, PMC7002296](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7002296)
- **Issar et al. (bioRxiv):** a neural-network classifier outputs a per-waveform spike likelihood, and the cutoff on that likelihood acts as a tunable stringency knob. In most sessions, removing low-likelihood waveforms improved decoding relative to raw threshold crossings, and the benefit grew with time since implant. This is evidence that the optimum is not "keep everything" once noise events become common. — [Issar et al., bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/node/1136608)
- **Li & Li 2017 (Front Neurosci 11:406):** leftover events that match no template add decoding accuracy, but once that is controlled for, sorting still helped. — [Frontiers 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2017.00406/full)
- **Unverified:** a search-engine summary reports an auditory-cortex study that grid-searched threshold and bin size and found the best decoding threshold near 2 SD, below the 3–5 SD typical for sorting. The record was inaccessible (HTTP 403), so authors and numbers are unconfirmed. — [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/42j06Jw4)

### Inferences
- In both BMI and hippocampal decoding, decoding gains from spikes that a quality-maximizing pipeline would drop. A sorter or curation threshold tuned for unit isolation (high SNR, low contamination) is therefore expected to sit *above* the decoding-optimal sensitivity. For the user's harness, a KS4 configuration that yields more, lower-quality units, or more total assigned spikes, can legitimately score better on position decoding while scoring worse on isolation metrics. That is not a contradiction.
- The decoding-vs-threshold curve is flat near its optimum (Christie: -3 to -5 x RMS similar). Small sensitivity differences between KS4 and KS2.5 settings may change decoding by less than session-to-session variance, so effect sizes need paired, cross-validated comparisons.
- Kloosterman's result that sorted clusters *without quality criteria* matched clusterless decoding suggests the decoding loss comes mostly from *excluding* units and spikes, not from misassigning them. For a decoding score, including all clusters (including MUA-labelled ones) is likely close to optimal. For tuning, place-field or correlation scores it is not.
- The noise floor puts a lower bound on the optimum. Issar et al. show that when noise events become common (old implants), filtering them out helps. The optimum sits where marginal spikes still carry position-modulated signal on average.

### Gaps
- I found no hippocampal study that sweeps detection threshold (or Kilosort `Th_learned`/`Th_universal`) against position-decoding error, and no study comparing KS4 with KS2.5 on decoding.
- Fraser et al. 2009 and Deng et al. 2015: quantitative results not retrieved.

## 3. Information-theoretic framing

### Takeaway
Information-theoretic work agrees that sorting errors lose information. Whether pooling (no sorting) loses much depends on how differently the pooled neurons are tuned. The one capacity analysis that ranks error types (Goodman & Johnson) finds FPs far more damaging than FNs or mislabels for channel capacity. That is the opposite emphasis from Pazienti & Grün's synchrony result, which shows the ranking of error types depends on the analysis.

### Cited Findings
- **Won, Tiesinga, Henriquez & Wolf 2007 (J Neural Eng), abstract/summary-level:** cosine-tuned model neurons, mutual information (MI) with movement direction. Reported spike-sorting error rates reduce MI significantly for single-unit spike trains. Information in a small population is maximised when responses are sorted and tuning is diverse. Unsorted (pooled) activity retained 79–92% of the sorted information under moderate conditions. The loss from pooling depends on tuning differences between pooled neurons and on the sorting error rate. An earlier conference version reported that under 10% error could be tolerated before information fell to half its error-free maximum. — [Won dissertation, Duke](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/e54d03f8-11f7-4b19-8dd1-cc4aee96a666/full); [Duke scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/776464)
- **Goodman & Johnson 2008 (BMC Neurosci poster; ICASSP 2008; Network: Comput Neural Syst), vector-Poisson channel model:** "False positives are the most crucial type of error, severely degrading the capacity, even when the error rate is extremely small. Missed spikes also degrade the capacity, but the effect is only linear[]." FPs reduce capacity far more than missed or mislabelled spikes. Their conclusion: for neural *control*, the optimal sorter "should be intentionally biased to reduce the rate of false positives" even though it does not preserve spike-train statistics, whereas accurate reconstruction matters for experimental analysis. — [Goodman & Johnson, BMC Neurosci 9(S1):P122](https://bmcneurosci.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2202-9-S1-P122)
- **Laquitaine et al. 2024 (bioRxiv 10.1101/2024.12.04.626805); detailed biophysical rat S1 column with a simulated Neuropixels-like probe, 8 stimulus directions, manifold "information capacity"; sorters KS1, 2, 2.5, 3, 4 and Herdingspikes:** undersampling (randomly dropping ground-truth neurons to match KS4's yield) had no measurable effect on capacity (all p>0.47). *Biased* sampling (sorted units over-representing certain cell types, rates, layers or spike extents) cut capacity by about 21%. Overall, sorted populations lost about 50% of capacity. Capacity from "good" units was 37% below ground truth, and degraded units (merged or incomplete) were a further 12% below good units, averaged across KS4, KS2.5, KS1 and Herdingspikes. Sorting accuracy was ten times lower in the biophysical model than in simpler synthetic data. KS3 and KS4 produced no false-positive units, unlike older Kilosort versions. — [Laquitaine et al. 2024 bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.12.04.626805.full)
- **Ghahari & Eden 2025:** in hippocampal decoding, the per-spike entropy reduction was larger under clusterless than sorted models for 89% of spikes. Low-amplitude spikes (<200 µV) carried little information *in isolation*, but amplitude did not simply predict *incremental* information. — [Ghahari & Eden 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06280v1)

### Inferences
- These framings give a principled criterion: a marginal unit or spike set should be included if its incremental information (conditional on the rest of the population) exceeds the information it destroys by contaminating other units. Ghahari & Eden's "incremental entropy reduction" is a direct, computable version of this for position decoding and could be applied per cluster in the user's harness.
- Laquitaine et al. imply that *which* units a sorter finds (selection bias) matters more than *how many*. A sensitivity increase that recovers under-represented classes (e.g. low-amplitude or sparse-firing cells such as CA1 pyramidal cells with small place fields) may be worth more than its raw unit count suggests.
- The FP-vs-FN conflict (Goodman & Johnson vs Pazienti & Grün) reflects different objectives: capacity of a rate channel vs significance of coincidences. Neither generalises to every analysis.

### Gaps
- I found no MI-versus-detection-threshold curve for real hippocampal or cortical data.
- Laquitaine et al. per-sorter (KS4 vs KS2.5) capacity numbers were not in the parts of the text I could read. I also could not confirm whether including multi-units increased capacity.

## 4. Population size: do many marginal units help or hurt population analyses?

### Takeaway
For population-level quantities (latent trajectories, low-dimensional dynamics, linear decoding), adding imperfectly isolated units or using multi-unit channels generally preserves conclusions. Random-projection arguments explain why, and the distortion shrinks as channel count grows. The failure cases are anticorrelated tuning within a channel, high-dimensional or long-duration structure, and dense probes where channels are not independent projections. Analyses that need per-neuron identity (tuning, cell type, pairwise timing) do not share this tolerance.

### Cited Findings
- **Trautmann et al. 2019:** distortion of population trajectories under random projection scales as ε² ∝ (1/M)·ln(C·T/τ), where M is channel count, C the number of conditions, T the trajectory duration and τ the correlation timescale. Multi-unit activity works best with many channels, few conditions, short trajectories (under a few seconds), smooth dynamics and reasonable SNR. "Large distortions are only observed when neurons on each electrode are pathologically anti-correlated." Distortion grows only modestly with neurons per channel. — [Trautmann et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7002296)
- Trautmann et al., Neuropixels: about 350 neurons per session, about 150–250 isolatable (Kilosort plus Phy). Trajectories were similar but distortion was larger than for Utah arrays, because neighbouring channels see the same neurons and so give "fewer independent projections." Motor cortex needs 10–15 dimensions to capture 90% of variance; higher-dimensional areas may need more channels. Their stated limits: not for stimulus selectivity, inter-neuron spike timing or cell typing; drift of "10's of microns" matters. — [Trautmann et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7002296)
- Trautmann et al.: hash-only spikes and randomly recombined units also reproduced the Ames et al. population result. — [Trautmann et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7002296)
- **Laquitaine et al. 2024:** randomly removing neurons (pure undersampling) did not reduce stimulus-discrimination capacity, but cell-type-biased selection did. KS2–4 preserved the low-dimensional shape of stimulus responses while reducing discriminability. — [Laquitaine et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.12.04.626805.full)
- **Kloosterman et al. 2014:** in CA1, the clusterless advantage over sorting was bigger for single electrodes (38%) than tetrodes (14%), so the cost of strict sorting is largest when isolation is hardest. — [Kloosterman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3921373)
- **Ghahari & Eden 2025:** clusterless gains concentrated on parts of the track covered by few sortable neurons. Where sortable neurons were dense, sorted and clusterless decoding were similar. — [Ghahari & Eden 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06280v1)

### Inferences
- For hippocampal position decoding, extra marginal units help most where place-field coverage by good units is sparse. The decoding benefit of a more sensitive KS4 setting should show up as reduced error in poorly covered track segments, and per-position error breakdowns would test this.
- On dense probes (Neuropixels-type) a marginal unit can be a split or duplicate of a well-isolated neighbour. Such a unit adds no independent projection while double-counting spikes, which biases Bayesian decoders that assume independence (overconfident posteriors) and inflates correlations. This is a distinct cost of over-splitting from FP noise.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Stevenson & Kording 2011 (Nat Neurosci, "How advances in neural recording affect data analysis") or Stringer/Pachitariu population papers on tolerance to imperfect sorting, so no claims from them are included.
- I found no quantitative study of how over-splitting (duplicated units) specifically biases Bayesian position-decoding calibration.

## 5. Explicit recommendations to choose sorting parameters by the downstream analysis

### Takeaway
Several authors explicitly argue that the sorter's operating point should depend on the use: biased toward fewer FPs for control (Goodman & Johnson), keep hash and unsorted spikes for decoding (Kloosterman; Todorova; Christie), joint or ensemble sorting for correlations (Ventura & Gerkin). Practical methods exist (decoding-driven model selection; per-channel sort-or-not decided by decoding; likelihood cutoffs tuned on decoding), but I found no standard protocol that sweeps modern-sorter hyperparameters against downstream metrics.

### Cited Findings
- Goodman & Johnson: "Although accurate spike train reconstruction is important for experimental analysis, for neural control applications the optimal spike sorter should be intentionally biased to reduce the rate of false positives." — [BMC Neurosci 2008 poster](https://bmcneurosci.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2202-9-S1-P122)
- **Wood et al. 2004 (EMBS/TBME):** an automated sorter whose model selection was driven by decoding performance on motor-cortex data decoded better than human expert sorts. — [Wood et al. EMBS 2004](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~fwood/papers/Wood-EMBS-2004.pdf); [Wood et al. TBME 2004](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~fwood/papers/Wood-TBME-2004.pdf)
- Christie et al. recommend Ventura's algorithm for partial sorting because it "determines if units should be unsorted or sorted based on what leads to the highest decoder performance." They also recommend RMS-relative (not fixed-voltage) thresholds set by array noise level. — [Christie et al. 2015](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0ww0z6xm)
- Ventura & Gerkin argue correlation studies need a "new spike-sorting paradigm" that keeps waveforms and sorts jointly with the spiking model, rather than reusing hard assignments made for single-unit work. — [Ventura & Gerkin 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3358902)
- Trautmann et al. recommend threshold crossings for population-dynamics questions and sorting for single-neuron questions. — [Trautmann et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7002296)
- Issar et al.: a tunable per-waveform likelihood cutoff, chosen by decoding, beat raw thresholding in most sessions. — [Issar et al. bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/node/1136608)

### Inferences
- A practical recipe for the user's harness, built on the methods above:
  1. Define the objective per analysis: cross-validated position-decoding error and posterior calibration (HPD coverage, as in Ghahari & Eden) for decoding; ground-truth- or hybrid-based contamination and FN rates for tuning and correlation analyses.
  2. Sweep sensitivity settings (e.g. KS4 `Th_universal`/`Th_learned`, KS2.5 `Th`, and post-hoc curation cutoffs) and plot each objective against the setting.
  3. Expect the decoding optimum at higher sensitivity (more units and spikes, including MUA) than the isolation optimum. Choose the setting per analysis, or choose one sort and apply different unit-inclusion filters downstream: all clusters for decoding, strict isolation for place fields, CCGs and synchrony.
  4. Use a clusterless decoder on the same data as an upper reference. The gap between the sorted decoder and clusterless measures how much information the sort discards (Kloosterman; Ghahari & Eden).
- Because decoding and isolation optima differ, a single composite "sorter quality" score that mixes them can hide the trade-off. Reporting the two separately is better supported by this literature.

### Gaps
- I found no published head-to-head study that tunes Kilosort (any version) hyperparameters against downstream decoding or correlation metrics.
- I found no hippocampal-specific guidance on what contamination level is acceptable for place-field or theta-sequence analyses.
